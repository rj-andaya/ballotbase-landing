Bill Tracking Starts Too Late for Local Government. Here Is What Comes First.

A bill tracker starts at the bill number. For a city, county or special district, the fight starts earlier. One Oregon rural water bill shows every signal a tracker misses.

Every city, county and special district that watches its state legislature does it the same way. Somebody in the manager's office or the government affairs shop keeps a list of bills. The list comes from the state's own site, the league's weekly tracker, or a paid tool. When a bill on the list moves, an email goes out. When the session ends, the list gets archived.

The list works. It is also structurally late, and the reason is simple: every one of those tools is keyed on a bill number. Nothing exists in the system until a bill is filed. But for the people whose job is to defend a jurisdiction, the fight almost never starts at the bill number.

Here is what it looks like on one real bill.

A rural water bill, from the official record

In January 2025, the Oregon House took up HB 2168, a bill directing the OSU Extension Service to stand up an assistance program for households on private wells and septic systems, with appropriations to the Water Resources Department's well repair fund and to DEQ's septic loan program. [1] It is not a headline bill. It is exactly the kind of bill that touches a rural county's residents, a water district's service area, and a city's groundwater, without ever mentioning any of them by name.

The timeline in the legislative record: [1]

  • January 10: printed as introduced.
  • January 13: first reading.
  • January 17: referred to Agriculture, Land Use, Natural Resources and Water. The public hearing was posted the same day.
  • January 22: public hearing. Five days after the posting.
  • January 29: work session posted. February 3: work session held, passed out of committee to Ways and Means.
  • June 27: in committee upon adjournment. The bill died in Ways and Means without a floor vote.

Twenty-six pieces of testimony were filed for the January 22 hearing: 21 in support, 1 opposed, 4 neutral, using the position each submitter selected themselves. [2] The supporters included a regional council of governments, a community development lender, a statewide environmental council, a farm advocacy group, the League of Women Voters, and four septic contractors. DEQ testified neutral.

In the hearing itself, witnesses told the committee that groundwater levels in the Bend and Redmond area of the Deschutes Basin had declined by 20 feet in two decades, that more than 200 rural Deschutes County residents were dealing with dry wells, and that the money DEQ already had "hasn't even begun to address the total need in Central Oregon." [3]

Now the part that matters for anyone who runs a jurisdiction's legislative program: no city and no county government appears anywhere in that testimony record. The closest thing to a local government voice was the regional council of governments. The bill was about groundwater under specific communities, witnesses named those communities in the hearing room, and the communities themselves were not in the record.

That is not a criticism of anyone. It is what a five-day notice window does to an office with one person watching the legislature and a council meeting on the same night.

The four signals that came before, or after, the bill number

Look at the sequence again and count the moments where a bill tracker would have helped, versus the moments where it could not.

1. The hearing notice. Posted January 17, heard January 22. A tracker will email you when the hearing is posted. What it will not do is tell you that five days is short, that your jurisdiction has no testimony on file, or that four contractors and an environmental council already do. The alert is the same alert for every bill on the list. The one that matters looks identical to the twenty that do not.

2. The testimony gap. After the hearing, the record shows exactly who spoke for whom. A tracker does not read that record. So the fact that a bill about your groundwater was heard without you is invisible until somebody on council reads about it in the paper, or until the next session, when the same bill comes back.

3. The draft before the bill. In Oregon, many bills begin as Legislative Concepts, drafts that appear as exhibits on interim committee agendas weeks or months before they are filed with a number. [4] That is the earliest public signal of what is coming. It lives on committee agendas, not in any bill tracker, because there is no bill yet to track.

4. The registration after the bill died. HB 2168 died in June 2025. On August 20, 2026, fourteen months later, the community development lender that had testified in support registered a new lobbyist with the Oregon Government Ethics Commission, and the three lobbyists it had registered in January 2026 now show as terminated. [5] We do not know what that registration is for, and neither does anyone else outside that organization. The registration is the fact. But if you are the one watching rural water for a county, a supporter of a dead water bill re-staffing its lobbying six months before the next long session is a fact you want the morning it is published, not when the bill is reintroduced.

Four signals. A bill tracker delivers one of them, and delivers it undifferentiated.

What "before, during, and after" means for a jurisdiction

The bill tracker covers the middle of a bill's life: filed, referred, heard, amended, voted. That is table stakes and every tool has it. The parts that decide whether a jurisdiction is surprised are the two ends.

Before is the draft on an interim agenda, the executive order briefed to a committee, the hearing posted with short notice, the lobbyist registration on your issue. It is the layer where the organizations with a full-time Capitol presence already operate, and where a one-person government affairs office is structurally blind.

After is the record: who testified and where they stood, what was said in the room, how each member voted, and what the same organizations did next. It is what lets you walk into council with "here is who was in the room and here is who was not," instead of "the bill died."

Neither end requires anything secret. All of it is in the state's own systems: the legislature's measure and testimony records, the committee agendas, the ethics commission's registration lists. The problem was never access. The problem is that those systems do not talk to each other and nobody in a city manager's office has time to read them daily.
